The Job, the Check, and the Bill

Prompt Daily, Friday, October 9, 2026. The episode covers Thursday, October 8. Maya and Ravi are A.I. voices. Prompt Daily is produced by LumeGrid.

The thread is simple. An A.I. is being given real work. Who gives it permission, who checks the work, and who pays?

Google. Sharon Prosser's Google Cloud post, dated October 8, says the new Gemini agent was announced that day. Small and midsize business customers are in early access, and access for all customers is coming soon. No date and no consumer price are in that post. Personal Gemini account access is unconfirmed. Product details are from Thomas Kurian's keynote post, adapted from the Gemini at Work keynote. Google says the agent can keep working in the cloud after the laptop closes, across a phone, a laptop, Slack, Workspace, and Microsoft 365. Google's example is an events team: ask for the usual regional leads, and the agent uses the chat and the last thread, checks calendars, and starts the email. A research job can run from notes to a spreadsheet model to a deck. A coworker role can have its own account, email, calendar, Drive, and audit log, and a spend cap can pause it. The agent can route work to Claude as well as to Google's models. The keynote page date has displayed both October 8 and October 9 across reads. The Thursday announcement in this episode relies on Prosser's October 8 post.

Goodfire, Thursday. For Kimi K3, probes look at internal activations and can hand a suspicious step to another model, which can block a tool call before it runs. Goodfire says this is on its production stack, not that every Kimi service switched it on. The monitor estimates risk from internal signals. On tests Goodfire built, the company reports about 93 percent of harmful sessions caught while interrupting about 5.5 percent of benign ones. The episode says that in plain language, most and some. FAR.AI's quoted test was two days of static chat attacks. Some individual attempts still worked. The episode asks whether a block holds when the attacker changes plan.

Anthropic, Thursday. OSS Scanner is free and opt in for eligible projects with critical impact. The reports are written by the model, with no human review first, and Anthropic says some will be wrong. A candidate is not a verified vulnerability. A way to reproduce the bug, an explanation, and sometimes a patch are the offer. The company's scanner page says it will not start a 90-day disclosure clock on those unchecked findings. If Anthropic later validates one through human review and notifies the maintainer, the clock can start from that notice.

The White House, Thursday, at its science summit. The wide package is described as over 6 billion dollars across government, industry, academia, and philanthropy. Inside that, 2.4 billion dollars is a commitment of tools and compute credits from eleven companies for the Genesis Mission. The fact sheet's label for those tools is super intelligence. Anthropic's own Thursday post puts its slice at 150 million dollars over three years in Claude, Claude Code, and API credits, for several hundred projects and more than 15 agencies, including NASA and the National Institutes of Health, plus training and startup help. That is a commitment of credits and support. Michael Kratsios framed the day as a historic turning point for American science. The episode paraphrases that rhetoric. The test it uses is whether researchers actually use the tools.
